Who Bears Responsibility When Accessing Care: Mental Health Service Barriers at Harvard

What If Getting Help Requires the Energy You No Longer Have?

By Evelyn Mao

As a graduate student at Harvard Medical School who was previously diagnosed with PTSD and depression, I had been considering getting help from the Counseling and Mental Health Services (CAMHS) since the first day of the semester. However, it took me almost a month to have an initial consultation phone call with them and another week to get referred and schedule a meeting with a therapist off campus, only because students who seek help from CAMHS for the first time are required to have the mandatory 30-minute telephone consult, and the earliest appointment I could schedule was 2 weeks later. I missed the first call, for which they didn’t send out any reminders, so it took me another 2 weeks to reschedule and talk to the consultant, even though I messaged them that I could send them all my past reports through the HUHS Patient Portal. They still rejected my proposal to skip the phone call process, though I requested an immediate therapy intervention.

This experience made me question how much responsibility mental health procedures place on Harvard students who are already seeking help. In this paper, I will examine three main sources about categorization of administrative barriers, cognitive decline in depression, and previous problems in CAMHS acknowledged by Harvard University, using Harvard’s 2020 report as additional material. I argue that Harvard’s mental health service access depended too much on students’ capacity to meet them.

To examine this problem, I draw on Metzl and Hansen’s concept of structural competency from our class reading that explores how institutional and social conditions shape care access. (Metzl and Hansen, 2014) Whether students finished the help-seeking procedures shouldn’t be merely explained by whether students have made enough effort or not. What needs to be focused on is whether the system made help-seeking more difficult.

According to Administrative Burden: Learning, Psychological, and Compliance Costs in Citizen-state Interactions, Moynihan and colleagues conceptualize administrative barriers in terms of learning costs, which involve the ability to understand rules and requirements; compliance costs, which involve meeting requirements; and psychological costs, which involve undertaking more stress, anxiety, and loss of autonomy. (Moynihan et al., 2014) The authors investigate how policies shape responsibility distribution between institutions and individuals through analysis of Wisconsin’s medical policy change. Though this paper examines citizen-state interaction rather than university mental health service access, it helps illustrate how responsibility for obtaining care is distributed in a system. The frame proposed by this paper implies that receiving service is also mutual and requires labor from receivers. Applied to CAMHS, it questions who should be responsible for coordinating access to care and whether students with mental health issues need to undertake work necessary to obtain help. However, it doesn’t explain why CAMHS adopts certain procedures or differences in service users’ feedback.

During the Harvard orientation week, I found that the introduction of CAMHS and the medical insurance was simply brushed over. For students who need specific mental health support, they need to search online for all the services they can get and the specific rules of the medical system at Harvard, rather than being assigned and informed by a personal counselor. While most of the general information could be found on CAMHS’s website, they are all separated and hidden in different tabs that require a huge amount of time to explore. Therefore, the threshold of learning and understanding the rules of CAMHS requires extra time and energy.

Moreover, the procedure in the help-seeking process at CAMHS is highly dependent on students’ capacity to organize and sustain, expecting students to initiate help-seeking, manage their time to make space for meetings, arrive on time, reach out to off-campus therapists, and follow advice from therapists on their own, etc. According to the CAMHS website’s FAQ section on how many providers students should reach out to: “the initial (therapist) search is like ‘casting a wide net,’ and it is best to identify a minimum of 5 therapists and reach out to all of them, even if you are not sure whether they will be the right fit” (Harvard University, 2026), highlighting that contacting off-campus therapists and evaluating whether the therapist is a good fit is the student’s responsibility.

However, students with mental health issues often have trouble following procedures and staying executive in the help-seeking process. According to Rock et al. in Cognitive Impairment in Depression, their systematic review and meta-analysis found “significant moderate cognitive deficits in executive function, memory and attention in patients with depression relative to controls”, and those cognitive deviation comparing with the control group still exists in patients in remitted states. (Rock et al., 2014) Though this investigation doesn’t focus on Harvard students or how depression might affect care help-seeking, a problem that can be inferred is that students with depression or similar symptoms might forget meeting times more easily and find it more difficult to reach out to off-campus therapists by themselves. It can also be inferred that the same administrative requirement might have different effects on different students.

Beyond the learning cost and compliance cost, what’s more concerning is the psychological cost. Uncertainty about the response from either CAMHS or outside resources can make students more anxious and stressed. They might also feel frustration and helplessness if the off-campus therapist doesn’t fit their needs, since they spent a lot of sunk cost reaching out to them. In my case, being asked to reschedule a phone call is very frustrating and almost made me give up help-seeking. Some will also feel a sense of shame, disrespect, and stigma when they talk about their personal trauma with multiple off-campus therapists they were referred to and are unfamiliar with, especially since CAMHS recommends “net casting.”

In fact, there are a lot of students facing the same barriers. According to the recent 2025 Healthy Minds Survey: Harvard University National Comparison Report, which covers mental health status, sense of belonging, health services, and accessibility barriers at Harvard in 2025, Harvard’s institutional data suggests that among Harvard students who received healthcare services but still reported receiving fewer services than needed, 38% mention they don’t have enough time to get services, while 21% mention difficulty finding available appointments (Harvard University, 2025), implying how time-consuming the procedures might be and how limited mental health resources are at Harvard. But it shows a possibility that needs to be further researched, as it can be inferred that the mental health system's design might not align with Harvard students' lifestyles, who may already face pressure from schoolwork and struggle to balance study and health. However, this resource doesn’t identify which specific procedure consumes more time or whether students’ time was taken up by other things in their lives.

A similar report conducted by Harvard in 2020, using synthesis method, focus group discussion, and data comparison, also mentions existing problems with uncertainty of off-campus resource referral, which might impact help-seeking. In the Report of the Task Force on Managing Student Mental Health, Harvard mentions, “ the availability of mental health services across the country lags significantly behind demand, and the Boston area is no exception”.Things like off-campus institutions not taking new patients, not replying to students’ phone calls, or not fitting students' needs happen, and Harvard admitted that “For a student in distress, encountering such hurdles could lead them to give up on finding help.” (Harvard University, 2020)

Taken together, Moynihan et al., Rock et al., and the 2025 mental health service report of Harvard all suggest that mental health service acquisition not only depends on the availability of service, but also on students’ capacity to navigate the process of obtaining them. These materials reveal a mismatch between students’ capacity and the expectations of CAMHS for students who demand help. Procedures, such as telephone counseling, navigating the website to find information and schedule appointments, and reaching out and hearing back from off-campus resources, are intended to make counseling more efficient and personalized, but instead become barriers that are time- and energy-consuming. These three materials show a tension: explaining responsibility allocation only based on cognitive barriers will attribute responsibility to students, while analyzing procedures alone might overlook individual differences.

From a biosocial perspective, even though the 2 Harvard mental health service reports acknowledge limits within their mental health system, their future research focus should shift more from their service user feedback to who their service users are, analyzing how much time they spend on schoolwork, commuting, socializing, and personalizing services more on students’ current health state, mood, memory, and attention. In other words, CAMHS’s room for improvement needs to focus more on whether their service considers the help-seeker’s capacity, instead of service efficiency.

For my broader project, this review suggests that students’ capacity should be one of the main factors when considering mental health service accessibility. However, existing materials don’t establish how experience differs between students or which step creates the most burdens. Therefore, I would like to interview CAMHS service users at Harvard and develop a more comprehensive understanding of individuals’ pain points, needs, capacities, and concerns when it comes to accessing mental health services. I would also like to investigate the flow of students’ insurance, exploring whether it’s feasible for individuals to get assigned a personal health advisor who checks in with students weekly.
