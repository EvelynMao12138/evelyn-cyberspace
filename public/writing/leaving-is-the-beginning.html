For Some, Leaving an Abusive Partner Is Only the Beginning

By Evelyn Mao

“My husband controlled my money and everything. He hit me multiple times until one day, I could no longer stand it,” said Joan, a 61-year-old domestic violence survivor with arthritis, whom I met at Women’s Lunch Place (WLP), a shelter and public lunch place in Boston for women experiencing homelessness and poverty.

“I left the house I had taken care of for years, and I was standing in the middle of the street wondering where I should go next. I wasn’t able to find a job. I had slept on streets or stayed over at my friend’s place before I found this place,” she said.

Her friend, Melissa, 58 years old, with diabetes, also told me that she didn’t have a single penny in her pocket when she left her partner.

As a survivor of intimate partner violence, I know how difficult it is to leave an abusive partner. I was able to leave, but life can be much harder when someone has no money to leave and no family to turn to, especially for older women and women with disabilities, who have fewer options for stable work and are often overlooked in the discussion of domestic violence.

For them, leaving is a bolder and more complicated decision. “ I've seen that happen where somebody uses the other person's credit and gets the other person deeply in debt with no means to repay it, ”said Marianna Yang, a clinical instructor at Harvard Law School’s Family Justice Clinic, who works with intimate partner violence survivors, telling me that financial abuse from a partner can leave women with few housing options. She told me that legal remedies sometimes can’t change much and the most urgent thing for survivors is to figure out how to get away safely.

Joan’s case isn’t isolated. In fact, according to WLP’s report in 2021, 58% of their service users have experienced domestic violence, and 48% have trouble paying critical bills. A more recent report in 2025 mentions that at least 90% of the service users at WLP experienced some form of trauma, and 66% of their guests are above the age of 50.

Joan received help from a shelter. Unfortunately, not everyone does. According to GBH’s report, Massachusetts reduced a Department of Public Health budget account supporting domestic violence and sexual assault for 2026 by more than $6 million, approximately 8%. Shelters like Casa Myrna said its federal VOCA funding has dropped by 38%, about $600,000. This will directly impact their homelessness prevention, legal advocacy, crisis intervention, and mental health counselling services. GBH’s report tells a story about how Casa Myrna helped a mother of six flee a controlling abuser with resources like hotels, food, and clothes. Now the organization’s CEO worries that after the financial cut, it won't be able to support other survivors with the same help. Last year, domestic violence shelters in Massachusetts received 873 unfulfilled requests for help in one day; 73% were requesting housing.

“ Yes, we are currently very short on beds, but we will make sure to accommodate everyone as best as we can,” confirmed Gail, Casa Myrna’s communications officer, through email.

This brings up a problem: when resources are scarce, who will decide who gets resources first and who deserves more assistance? How frontline staff respond can completely change survivor’s resources access and willingness seeking help, yet not every staff is impartial.

For Anna K, a Google reviewer, the answer to the latter question appear to be individuals that are well-behaved enough. She commented on WLP’s Google Maps page about her unpleasant experience that women with disabilities and mental health issues were made to feel unwelcome here. She also mentioned that those who try to please staff will get more preferential treatment, while the staff sometimes are impatient and disrespectful to her. “They also need to HAVE A LOT MORE UNDERSTANDING. PEOPLE ARE COMING HERE WITH A LOT OF TRAUMA. MANY WITH DV AND SA.”

Another reviewer, Dana B., commented under Casa Myrna’s Google Maps page that she had the police referral when calling Casa Myrna, but the staff questioned why the police couldn’t find a place for her themselves. “These people think domestic violence is a game. I don't understand; they give them funds to help the victim, and what they do is restrict the aid they think they give it to them for themselves, and they yell at you when you are asking for something valid for him you deserve to give.”

These reviews reflect individual’s experiences. Rather than eplicitly rejecting assistance, staffs seems like brushing them off with an impatient attitude. For survivors who have trauma and disabilities, being treated disrespectfully can make their act of asking for help feel humiliating. Yet dignity is something promised on the shelter’s website.

Moreover, the resources may be more likely to go to women who look “normal” or “compliant”, but for domestic violence survivors, it is hard to be expected to act completely calm and cooperative. Yang told me that PTSD caused by domestic violence can make survivors struggle to sound consistent, express needs and regulate emotions. Yet some of their angers were indeed caused by administrative barriers due to unnecessary steps in the intake process.

It’s going to snow in Boston soon. While some of us will sip hot cocoa under warm blankets, there will be domestic violence and intimate partner violence survivors who can’t access a warm and safe place due to a shortage of shelter beds or their voices being unheard by shelter staff. I hope Massachusetts can fund more emergency hotel stays before snow falls that can enable a woman who fled from violence to make it through the night, whatever she has in her wallet. If there are no beds in shelter, a patient response and referral to another program can be very helpful.
